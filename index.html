Chapter 3: Data Privacy, Security, and Governance in AI-Enabled Practice

Why the other choices are wrong: State medical board licensing applies to individual clinicians, not to a practice's vendor data-sharing arrangements. There is no HIPAA requirement to publish vendor names publicly. There is no rule tying data-sharing eligibility to how long a vendor has existed.
Question 2
Correct answer: C. The minimum necessary standard requires limiting protected health information use and disclosure to what is reasonably needed for the specific intended purpose.
Why the other choices are wrong: Granting unrestricted full access regardless of function is precisely what this standard aims to prevent. There is no blanket 24-hour deletion rule tied to this standard. The standard applies broadly to protected health information, including electronic data, not only paper records.
Question 3
Correct answer: A. Published research has shown that de-identified datasets, especially rich health data, can sometimes be re-identified through linkage against other available data sources.
Why the other choices are wrong: De-identification is explicitly permitted and defined under HIPAA's Privacy Rule, not illegal. De-identified data does not automatically become public record and remains subject to a vendor's own data use terms. There is no date-based limit on when de-identification can be applied.
Question 4
Correct answer: D. This use is generally lawful because de-identified data falls outside HIPAA's core restrictions, but it raises a distinct ethical governance question about patient visibility into the practice.
Why the other choices are wrong: This practice is not universally illegal, since de-identified data is treated differently under HIPAA. Correct answer: B. AI-specific disclosure norms remain genuinely unsettled and variable, unlike HIPAA's more standardized and long-established core privacy and security framework.
Why the other choices are wrong: HIPAA does not ban AI use; it sets requirements for how patient data is handled. Question 12

Chapter 5: Ethical Principles and Algorithmic Bias in Clinical AI

Question 1
Correct answer: C. A model trained on a proxy label like healthcare cost can produce racially unequal predictions even without using a demographic variable directly, because the proxy itself is unevenly distributed by access to care.
Why the other choices are wrong: A privacy violation would be a data governance failure, not an explanation for systematically unequal predictive accuracy. Inconsistent clinician use would produce random variation rather than a directional bias tied to a demographic pattern. A software coding error would typically be caught during validation and would not selectively track a specific patient subgroup in the way documented bias cases have.
Question 2
Correct answer: D. Beneficence requires that a tool actually benefit the specific patients it is used on, not merely patients similar to those in its original validation study.
Why the other choices are wrong: Respecting a patient's right to make their own decisions is a separate concern from whether a tool works for them clinically. Protecting sensitive health information is unrelated to whether a validation population matches the deployment population. Paperwork practices around consent do not address whether the underlying tool actually improves outcomes for this patient group.
Question 3
Correct answer: B. Measurement bias occurs when the underlying input data is collected less accurately for one group, and oximetry's differential accuracy by skin pigmentation is a documented example of this input-level problem.
Why the other choices are wrong: Using a model in a setting different from where it was validated describes a mismatch in deployment context, not an input measurement problem. Insufficient representation of a group in training data is a separate issue from an instrument systematically mismeasuring a physiologic value. Training a model on a proxy outcome rather than the true clinical target describes a labeling problem, not a sensor accuracy problem.
Question 4
Correct answer: B. Individual clinician observations often surface patterns that a formal audit later confirms, and the appropriate path is to route the concern through the institutional process built to investigate and correct such issues.
Why the other choices are wrong: Dismissing a consistent observed pattern discards exactly the kind of early signal that has historically led to major bias discoveries. Abruptly halting use of a tool for the entire practice without investigation could remove a resource that is still helpful for many patients while addressing no underlying problem. Individually modifying a vendor's scoring algorithm is outside a clinician's expertise and role, and could introduce new, unvalidated errors.
Question 5
Correct answer: D. Aggregate accuracy can mask meaningfully worse performance for a smaller subgroup, which is exactly the pattern documented in several real algorithmic bias cases.
Why the other choices are wrong: High aggregate accuracy is entirely compatible with poor subgroup-specific performance, since an average can be pulled upward by strong performance in a majority group. Justice concerns apply whenever outcomes diverge by group, whether or not a demographic variable was an explicit model input. Fairness can drift over time as a population or data pipeline changes, so an initial validation does not guarantee permanent fairness.

Correct answer: D. Switching the model's target outcome from a cost-based proxy to a more direct measure of clinical need sharply reduced the disparity, showing the label choice, not the presence of demographic data, drove the bias.
Why the other choices are wrong: Removing lab values would likely reduce overall model accuracy without addressing the specific proxy-label mechanism causing the disparity. Question 7
Correct answer: B. Representation bias means a model's performance tends to degrade for presentations that were underrepresented during training, which has been a documented concern for dermatology image models and lighter-skewed datasets.
Why the other choices are wrong: Image classifiers are not inherently neutral to input distribution; their accuracy is shaped directly by what they were trained on. Models do not self-correct or recalibrate simply through exposure to new patients without a deliberate retraining or fine-tuning process. Question 8

Why the other choices are wrong: There is no general legal requirement mandating that protected variables remain in clinical prediction models. Why the other choices are wrong: No single technical metric can settle a question that fundamentally involves competing values about fairness and overall performance. Vendors have a commercial interest in the outcome and should not be the sole decision-maker on a question with direct patient equity implications. Chapter 2: How AI Systems Learn, and Where They Fail

Sort Each Failure Mode Into Its Category
Distribution Shift / Concept Drift: a model performing worse at a rural clinic than in its academic validation setting; a model's accuracy declining gradually over years as practice patterns change (concept drift); a model trained on a specialty referral population performing differently in a general primary care population (spectrum bias, a form of distribution shift). Overfitting / Dataset Bias: a model learning a hospital-specific imaging artifact instead of a real clinical signal (overfitting); a dermatology classifier showing lower accuracy on darker skin tones due to training data underrepresentation (dataset bias).
Find the Errors: Hallucination and Verification
Error 1: large language models have no built-in fact-checking system; they generate statistically plausible text without verifying it against a database. Error 2: hallucinated text is not reliably distinguishable by tone or fluency; it typically sounds just as confident and fluent as accurate text. Error 3: retrieval-augmented generation reduces but does not eliminate hallucination, so independent verification of citations, dosages, and other specific factual claims is still necessary even when it is used.

Chapter 3: Data Privacy, Security, and Governance in AI-Enabled Practice

Define the Governance Term
Business associate agreement: a required written contract between a covered entity and any vendor or entity that uses or discloses protected health information on the covered entity's behalf, setting out data protection obligations. Minimum necessary standard: the HIPAA requirement to limit the use, disclosure, and request of protected health information to what is reasonably necessary to accomplish a specific intended purpose. De-identification (Safe Harbor method): a HIPAA-recognized method of removing 18 specific categories of identifiers (such as names, geographic subdivisions smaller than a state, and dates more specific than a year) so that data is no longer considered protected health information. Data mapping: the governance practice of documenting exactly what patient data elements flow to a given vendor, in what form, how frequently, and for what purpose.
Vendor Risk Assessment Checklist
The correct order follows the five assessment categories in sequence as presented in the chapter: first confirm regulatory status and evidence, then review data handling specifics against the minimum necessary standard, then confirm the business associate agreement, then review security posture, then review business continuity and vendor stability terms, and finally designate internal practice readiness and ownership. Skipping the business associate agreement step before any data sharing is a critical safety and compliance failure, since HIPAA generally requires this agreement in place before protected health information is shared with the vendor.

Chapter 4: The Regulatory and Legal Landscape of Clinical AI

FDA Device Risk Classes
Class I: low risk, subject to the most limited regulatory controls. Class II: moderate risk, generally requires 510(k) premarket notification demonstrating substantial equivalence to a predicate device. Class III: highest risk, generally requires premarket approval involving a more extensive review of clinical data establishing reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness.
Sequence the 510(k) Clearance Logic
The correct order is: first, the manufacturer identifies an already legally marketed predicate device with a similar intended use and technological characteristics; second, the manufacturer submits a premarket notification demonstrating substantial equivalence to that predicate device; third, the FDA reviews the submission for reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness for the stated intended use; fourth, the device receives 510(k) clearance, which establishes a regulatory floor of demonstrated safety and effectiveness for its specific intended use, not a certification of superior accuracy or universal applicability.

Chapter 5: Ethical Principles and Algorithmic Bias in Clinical AI

Match Each Bias Mechanism to Its Description

Label bias matches the description of an outcome proxy, such as healthcare cost, that is unevenly distributed by group. Measurement bias matches the description of input data being collected less accurately for one group, as with pulse oximetry and darker skin tones. Deployment bias matches the description of a model being used in a setting or population different from the one it was validated on, such as an adult-validated sepsis model used in pediatrics.

Chapter 20: The Future of Family Medicine in an AI-Augmented Health System

Define the Key Terms
Augmentation (versus replacement) is the view, supported by current evidence, that AI tools assist and extend physician judgment rather than substitute for the physician's overall clinical role. AI literacy is a conceptual understanding of how a category of AI tool works, what data it was likely trained on, its known failure modes, and how to interpret its output appropriately, without requiring technical or coding expertise. The external validity gap is the phenomenon in which a tool's strong performance in its original validation setting does not automatically transfer to a different population or practice setting. The accountability gap is the unresolved question of who bears responsibility when an autonomous AI system's output is wrong, since current medical licensure and legal frameworks assume a human physician bears ultimate responsibility for a clinical decision.
